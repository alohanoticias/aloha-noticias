Why does the PlayStation controller have a triangle, circle, X and square? The designer explained

The four symbols aren't letters or decoration: each carries an idea, and even the colors sparked arguments inside Sony.

By Aloha TeamNovember 8, 2026, 09:50

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Almost everyone who has played a PlayStation knows by heart where the X is. Few know why it sits next to a triangle, a circle and a square, instead of letters like on rival consoles. The explanation comes from Teiyu Goto, the Sony designer who created the symbols, in a 2010 interview with the Japanese magazine Famitsu. I couldn't open the original interview: I read four texts that cite it, from Engadget, Gizmodo and twice from SlashGear, one from 2010 and one from 2025.

Each symbol had an idea

The triangle stands for point of view. According to Gizmodo, which quotes Goto, it suggests someone's head or direction, and the button is green. The square is a sheet of paper, something for accessing menus and documents, and it is pink. The circle and the X form the pair of decisions: yes and no. The circle is red and the X is blue. In Engadget's words, “the circle and x represent yes and no”, respectively.

Why not use letters?

Goto said the other companies at the time assigned letters or colors to the buttons. He wanted something easy to remember. According to Gizmodo, the triangle-circle-X-square combination came to him “immediately afterward” that idea. It's a rare case where a design solution fits in one sentence: instead of memorizing that “A” does this and “B” does that, the player associates a shape with a meaning.

The colors nobody understood

The designer himself admitted that the choice of colors caused puzzlement. In the Gizmodo version and the 2010 SlashGear one, Goto says people thought the colors were mixed up and that he had to insist to management that this was exactly what he wanted. The texts don't explain the reasoning behind each color, they only record the standoff.

The fight over the handles

The symbols were not the only battle. Goto also defended the handles that give the controller the shape we know. Gizmodo says management wanted something like the Super Nintendo controller and that Goto won the dispute. The 2025 SlashGear tells a more dramatic version: president Norio Ohga backed Goto, was a pilot and saw in the design something like a control stick, and there was a tense meeting in which he supposedly almost threw the model. That detail appears only in that source, so treat it as an account, not a confirmed fact.

What the sources don't answer

There are three questions the four texts leave open. None mentions the difference between Japan and the West when it comes to confirming and canceling (which button serves for which). None covers the dispute over whether the X is called “X” or “cross”. And none gives the date of creation: the 2010 SlashGear says Goto has handled the external design of Sony products for 17 years, which points to the early 1990s, before the PlayStation launched. There is also a divergence in the designer's name: three texts write Teiyu Goto and the 2025 SlashGear writes Taiyo Goto. I used the majority spelling. If you want the primary source, Goto's Famitsu interview was translated by the site 1UP and, according to the 2025 SlashGear, now survives only on the Wayback Machine.

And today?

More than three decades later, the four symbols remain on PS5 controllers practically unchanged. Goto's idea survived because it solved a simple problem: giving the player something learned in seconds and never forgotten. Next time you press X without thinking, remember that it was once, in theory, a “no”.

Sources: Engadget, Gizmodo, SlashGear, 2010 and SlashGear, 2025.
