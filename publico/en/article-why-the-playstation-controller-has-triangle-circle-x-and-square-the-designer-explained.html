Why does the PlayStation controller have a triangle, circle, X and square? The designer explained

To its designer, X meant “no.” We explain each symbol, compare the controller with Xbox and Nintendo and show how Sony turned that idea upside down on the PS5.

By João GabrielNovember 8, 2026, 09:50

What the symbols on the PlayStation controller mean (James Arnold channel, in English)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Almost everyone who has played on a PlayStation presses X without looking. Few people know that, to the man who designed the controller, that X meant “no.” The explanation came from Teiyu Goto, the Sony designer behind the symbols, in a 2010 interview with the Japanese magazine Famitsu. It was translated into English by 1UP, a site that has since shut down, and the translation can still be read on the Wayback Machine. Here I put together what Goto said, compare it with the Xbox and Nintendo controllers and show how Sony ended up going against his original idea.

Each symbol had a meaning

Goto thought of each shape as a mental shortcut. The triangle is a point of view, like someone's head or direction, so it is green. The square is a sheet of paper, the place for menus and documents, and got pink. The circle and the X are the decision pair: yes and no. The circle is red; the X, blue. Engadget and Gizmodo ran the same explanation at the time, both based on 1UP's translation.

Why not letters?

In the interview, Goto recalls that rivals at the time labeled buttons with letters or colors, and he wanted something players would learn at once. The four-shape idea came right after that. In my view, it was the smartest decision in the whole controller: instead of remembering that “A” does one thing and “B” another, the player links a shape to a meaning. As a bonus, Sony got a brand. The four symbols became an informal PlayStation logo, something A, B, X and Y never were for anyone.

Notice two traps. There is an X button on all three controllers, but it sits in a different place on each. And Nintendo puts “confirm” on the right, exactly where Xbox puts “back.” Anyone who switches consoles knows the feeling.

The X that became “yes”

In Japan, a circle (maru) marks something as correct, as when grading a test, and an X (batsu) marks it as wrong. That is why, from the first PlayStation on, Japanese games confirmed with circle and cancelled with X, as Goto intended. The West did the opposite: the X, which sits at the bottom and falls naturally under the thumb, became the confirm button.

Sony lived with both rules for 26 years. With the PS5, released in 2020, it made X the confirm button in every region, Japan included. Kotaku reported the change, and Anime News Network said Sony told the site AV Watch it wanted to avoid the confirm button being one thing in the console menu and another in games. In practice, the company gave up the meaning its own designer gave the buttons. I think it was the right call for a global market, but it is a curious twist: Goto's “no” became the whole world's “yes.”

The colors and the fight over the grips

The designer also admitted the colors puzzled people. In the translated interview, he says people thought they were mixed up and he had to insist to management. Another fight was over the grips. Management wanted a controller similar to the Super Nintendo's, to win over players of the rival console, and Goto argued for the shape with handles for both hands. SlashGear added a more dramatic account in 2025: Sony president Norio Ohga, a pilot, supposedly backed the design because it reminded him of a control yoke. That detail appears only in that source, so treat it as an account.

What is still unclear

Two things remain open. The exact date the symbols were created does not appear in the texts; SlashGear's 2010 piece says Goto had handled Sony's external design for 17 years, which points to the early 1990s, before the console launched in 1994. And the designer's name is spelled differently: most write Teiyu Goto, while SlashGear's 2025 piece writes Taiyo Goto. We went with the more common spelling.

And today?

More than three decades later, the four shapes are almost unchanged on the PS5's DualSense. The colors are gone from the buttons and two of them changed meaning, but Goto's core idea survived: give players something they learn in seconds and never forget.
