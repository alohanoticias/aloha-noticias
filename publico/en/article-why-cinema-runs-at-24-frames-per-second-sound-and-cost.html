Why does cinema run at 24 frames per second? The origin has more to do with sound and cost than with art

The number looks sacred, but it began as a deal struck in the late 1920s. What the sources confirm, where they disagree, and why TV never used 24.

By Aloha TeamNovember 6, 2026, 00:36

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

A minute of 35 mm film running at 24 frames per second uses roughly 27 metres of stock. That is simple arithmetic of our own: Wikipedia says the film travels 456 millimetres every second at that speed. Now picture paying for it at studio scale in the 1920s, and the question changes from “why 24?” to “why not fewer?”. The answer mixes sound, money and an agreement nobody planned as an eternal standard.

How it was before sound

Silent cinema had no fixed number. Cameras were hand-cranked, and the operator sped up or slowed down to suit the scene, according to Wikipedia, RedShark and Adobe. As for the range, the sources don't agree: Wikipedia gives rates of 16 to 24 frames per second, while RedShark and Adobe cite 14 to 26. In the theatre the projectionist could also tinker with the speed, and Wikipedia says studios often wanted cinemas to show silent films faster than they were shot.

One curiosity clears up a common mix-up. According to Wikipedia, Thomas Edison held that fewer than 46 images a second would strain the eyes. Projectors got around this with two- or three-blade shutters, which flashed each frame two or three times, for 48 or 72 flashes per second. In other words, frame rate and flicker rate are different things, and 24-frame cinema flickers far more than 24 times a second.

What sound changed

Once sound was recorded together with the picture, the speed had to stay constant. Wikipedia explains that variations were no longer tolerated because the ear is more sensitive than the eye to changes in frequency. According to the text, between 1927 and 1930, as studios updated their equipment, 24 frames became the standard for 35 mm sound.

Two sources, RedShark and Adobe, link the change to The Jazz Singer, from 1927. Wikipedia confirms the film premiered on October 6 that year and used the Vitaphone disc-based sound system, but its page says nothing about frame rate and says the film has only about two minutes of synchronised speech. So the film is a landmark of talking pictures, yet the adoption of 24 frames was gradual, and crediting it to a single work is a simplification.

Why 24, and not 22 or 26?

Here is where money comes in. RedShark, Adobe and StudioBinder say the choice balanced the cost of film stock against sound quality, without giving figures. Wikipedia uses the word “compromise”. StudioBinder, for its part, claims 24 emerged as the “optimal” rate after testing, without saying which tests. RedShark contradicts that idea by admitting that 24 “may not have been an artistic choice at first”. The version that best fits the sources is the agreement one: none of the texts points to a test proving that 24 was the best value for perception.

And what if it had been another number?

It did happen. The 70 mm Todd-AO filming system was born with 30 frames per second, 25% more, and debuted with Oklahoma! in October 1955. Wikipedia reports that the rate was changed to 24 after the second film, Around the World in 80 Days, from 1956, to make reduction prints in 35 mm easier. The Hobbit, from 2012, was released at 48 frames, according to RedShark, and the reaction was one of unease. The text says part of the audience found it looked like “reality TV”. Another text, from 8 Milímetros, says the whole trilogy used 48 and that many people found the look televisual, but it alone gives that version.

And television?

TV took another road. Wikipedia notes that analogue television used 50 fields per second in most of the world and 60 in countries such as the United States, Canada, Mexico and Japan. With the arrival of colour, 60 dropped by 0.1%, to 59.94. To get a film from 24 to 60, the method described is to repeat the odd frames twice and the even frames three times. Adobe says, in another passage, that TV and smartphones use 24, which contradicts Wikipedia's own figures and the rest of Adobe's text. That is why I am leaving that figure out.

RedShark says 24 may not have been an artistic choice at the start, but ended up becoming one. When someone tries another number, as with The Hobbit, part of the audience balks. How much of that is habit and how much is perception, the sources I read don't say.

Sources: Wikipedia: Frame rate, Wikipedia: 70 mm film, Wikipedia: The Jazz Singer, RedShark News, StudioBinder, Adobe, and 8 Milímetros (in Portuguese).
