Why do we eat popcorn at the movies? The answer involves the Great Depression and the physics of corn

Early movie theaters wanted popcorn nowhere near their seats. The 1929 crash, talking pictures and a kernel that turns into steam at 180 °C changed that.

By Aloha TeamNovember 12, 2026, 14:33

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Today it is hard to imagine a movie without the smell of popcorn at the door. But it was not always like that. In cinema's early years, popcorn was unwelcome: theater owners wanted to keep it away. The turning point came with an economic crisis, a technological change and a very particular trait of this kind of corn.

When movies wanted to look like theater

According to Smithsonian magazine, the first movie theaters in the United States tried to copy the feel of live theaters: expensive carpets, upholstered seats and an audience that behaved as if at the opera. Cheap, noisy food that dirtied the floor did not fit that image. Historian Andrew Smith, who wrote a book on the history of popcorn, told the magazine that theaters wanted nothing to do with it.

There was also a practical detail: until the late 1920s, films were silent and came with title cards on screen. Following the story meant being able to read, which limited the audience. With the arrival of talking pictures in 1927, theaters started drawing far more people, of all ages and social classes.

The crisis that filled the seats

In 1929 came the stock market crash and, with it, the Great Depression. Money was tight, but movies remained one of the cheapest forms of entertainment. So was popcorn: a bag cost 5 to 10 cents, a small luxury almost everyone could afford, according to Smithsonian.

Brazilian site AdoroCinema sums up why: corn was cheap and mass-produced, while other treats relied on expensive ingredients such as cocoa. No kitchen was needed either, just a cart with a hot plate. Vendors began parking outside theaters, and owners realized they were leaving money on the sidewalk. First they charged the vendors a fee; then they moved the machines into the lobby.

It worked. According to Smithsonian, by 1945 more than half of the popcorn eaten in the United States was consumed in movie theaters. The habit spread around the world along with Hollywood films and continues today: the concession stand is an important source of income for theaters.

Why the corn pops

Now for the physics. Not all corn becomes popcorn. Popping corn has a very tough outer hull, the pericarp, and holds a little water inside the kernel, between 13% and 14.5% of its weight, according to researchers quoted by ScienceAlert. When the kernel heats up, that water turns into steam but cannot escape: the hull works like a pressure cooker, and the starch inside softens.

In 2015, two French researchers, Emmanuel Virot and Alexandre Ponomarenko, filmed the process at high speed. At 170 °C, only about 34% of the kernels popped; at 180 °C, 96% did. That is the temperature at which pressure beats the hull, the starch expands all at once and cools into a foam, the white part we eat.

The study also explained the sound. The "pop" does not come from the hull cracking but from the steam escaping suddenly, which makes the cavities inside the kernel vibrate, as The Naked Scientists and NBC News explain. The authors compared the effect to a champagne cork. In other words, the most famous movie snack has not only a good story but some curious physics behind every kernel.

Sources: Smithsonian Magazine, AdoroCinema, NBC News / Live Science, ScienceAlert and The Naked Scientists.
