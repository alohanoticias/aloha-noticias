What the names in a movie's credits mean: gaffer, best boy, key grip and other jobs

The list that rolls after the last scene tells how a film was made. Some jobs have curious names, and some lines aren't even people's names.

By João GabrielNovember 27, 2026, 09:11

The jobs on a film set (Carol Moreira, in Portuguese)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

When the last scene ends and the house lights start coming up, almost everyone gets up. On screen, a list starts to roll that can run past ten minutes in big productions, with hundreds and sometimes thousands of names. For those who stay, some job titles stand out for their odd names: gaffer, best boy, key grip, dolly grip. Each one matches a concrete job, and understanding that list is a way of understanding how a film is made.

Why did credits get so long?

First, because films came to involve more people. A production with lots of visual effects spreads the work across several studios, each with dozens of artists, and all of them go on the list. Second, because credit is a professional right. In the United States, the guilds and unions of directors, writers, actors and technicians negotiate rules about who appears, in what order and with what prominence. A credit becomes a résumé: it is how a technician proves which films they worked on.

Gaffer and best boy: the lighting crew

The gaffer is the head of the lighting and electrical crew. The director of photography decides how the scene should look, and the gaffer decides which lamps to use, where to place each one and how to get power to them safely. The name comes from the theater: the old lighting crews used a hooked pole, similar to the fishing hook called a gaff, to adjust the lamps hanging above the stage.

The best boy is the gaffer's right hand. They handle the crew, the schedules, the equipment inventory and the power distribution across the set. There are two best boys on a film: the best boy electric, tied to lighting, and the best boy grip, tied to the next department. The title stays the same even when the job is held by a woman.

The grips: those who build, hold and move

The grip department is the one in charge of structure. The key grip, its head, coordinates the stands, scaffolding, fabrics and flags that shape or block the light the gaffer's crew turns on. They also handle the safety of everything hung or fastened up high. The dolly grip is the person who pushes the cart carrying the camera along tracks, and has to do it at a steady speed, stopping exactly on the agreed mark.

In Brazil, these jobs usually appear translated in the credits. In the guidelines of the Brazilian Society of Cinematographers, the gaffer is the chefe eletricista and the key grip is the chefe maquinista, both reporting to the director of photography.

Other jobs on the list

The script supervisor notes everything that happens in each take so that clothes, objects and gestures match when the scenes are edited together. The boom operator holds the pole with the microphone above the actors, out of frame. The colorist adjusts the color and contrast of each shot in post-production. Foley artists recreate footsteps, rustling clothes and other everyday sounds in the studio. And the list also includes those who make sure the crew eats, sleeps and gets to set: locations, transportation and catering.

The lines that aren't names

Some phrases in the credits have a story of their own. The notice "No Animals Were Harmed" is a certification mark of the American organization American Humane, which only allows its use when one of its representatives monitored the scenes with animals. The first film to receive the line was The Doberman Gang, from 1972. In Brazilian films made with federal public money, Ancine requires the logos of the agency and of the Audiovisual Sector Fund to appear in the credits, under Normative Instruction No. 130 of 2016.

Finally, a name that appears in dozens of films without ever having existed: Alan Smithee. It was the pseudonym created in 1968 by the Directors Guild of America for those who wanted to take their name off a work they considered mangled. It debuted on the western Death of a Gunfighter, from 1969, and official use ended in 2000. Next time, it's worth staying in your seat: besides these stories, many films hold an extra scene after the list.
