Did blowing into cartridges work? What Nintendo said and what really happened inside the console

The ritual of pulling the cartridge, blowing on it and putting it back marked a generation. The problem was rarely dust, and Nintendo itself asked people not to do it.

By Aloha TeamNovember 14, 2026, 10:41

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

A blinking screen, a game that won't start, scrambled graphics. Anyone who owned a cartridge console in the 1980s and 1990s knows what came next: pull the cartridge, blow on the contacts and put it back. Often the game worked. But the reason had little to do with blowing.

The problem wasn't dust

The habit was born with the NES, according to iFixit. The console used a different loading system from the Japanese Famicom: the cartridge was pushed in and then down. Its 72-pin connector had contacts bent so they would give a little and spring back when the cartridge came out, Mental Floss explains.

With use, those pins lost tension and contact became unreliable. The connectors also suffered from grime and oxidation. Most of the time, the fault was simply a bad connection between cartridge and console, not built-up dust.

There was a second point of failure. The NES had a lockout chip, the 10NES, which checked whether the cartridge was genuine and from the right region. If the pins tied to that chip didn't make good contact, the console refused the game and kept resetting, with the famous blinking red light, as The Silicon Underground explains.

Why it seemed to work

The simplest answer: to blow on it, you had to take the cartridge out and put it back in. That movement alone shifted the contacts and was often enough to make the game start. Blowing got the credit for something reseating had done, as Brazilian site Hardware.com.br puts it.

There is also a physical hypothesis. The moisture from your breath may have improved conductivity for a moment, enough for the game to load, according to The Silicon Underground. The problem is the long-term effect.

Why Nintendo said not to blow

Breath carries moisture and droplets of saliva onto the metal contacts. Over time, that speeds up corrosion, worsens contact and attracts more dirt, as Mental Floss and TecMundo explain. In other words, the trick that saved today's session could ruin the cartridge later.

Nintendo knew. Its official NES troubleshooting page began saying, around the mid-2000s, not to blow into cartridges or the console, and a similar warning was printed on the back of Nintendo 64 cartridges, according to iFixit. The company also sold an official cleaning kit for NES cartridges and the console slot, Digital Trends recalls.

So what is the right way? For anyone still playing on old consoles, TecMundo recommends cleaning the contacts with a cotton swab lightly dampened with isopropyl alcohol, letting it dry before inserting, and storing cartridges somewhere dry. If the problem persists across several cartridges, the fault may be the console's own connector, which sometimes needs replacing.

Sources: iFixit, Mental Floss, The Silicon Underground, Hardware.com.br, TecMundo and Digital Trends.
