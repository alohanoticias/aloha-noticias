SSD, HDD or microSD Express: which storage to use on PC, PS5, Xbox and Switch 2

100 GB games have made space a problem on every platform. Each console accepts a different kind of expansion, and buying the wrong one means a drive that won't run new games.

By João GabrielNovember 27, 2026, 13:44

How to choose external storage: HDD or SSD (KaBuM!, in Portuguese)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

A single big game today easily goes past 100 GB, and consoles ship with room for only a few of them. When it's time to expand, the question is always the same: buy a hard drive, which is cheap and big, or an SSD, which is fast and pricier? The answer depends less on price and more on where the drive will be used. PS5, Xbox Series, Switch 2 and PC accept different types, and in some cases the wrong model simply won't run new games.

What's the difference between an HDD and an SSD?

A hard drive stores data on magnetic platters spinning inside the case, while an arm with a read head moves to the right spot. Because it has mechanical parts, it is slower at finding scattered files and more sensitive to drops and bumps. Its advantage is cost per gigabyte, which remains much lower.

An SSD has no moving parts. Data sits on flash memory chips, and access is nearly instant. There are two main types. A SATA SSD, in the 2.5-inch format, reaches about 550 MB/s, which is the limit of the SATA connection. An NVMe SSD, in the M.2 stick format, connects directly to the PCIe bus and goes past 7,000 MB/s in fourth-generation models. For games that load the world as you walk through it, that difference shows up as shorter loading times and fewer hitches.

PS5: M.2 SSD inside, HDD outside

The PS5 has an internal bay for an extra SSD. Sony requires a fourth-generation M.2 NVMe PCIe model (Gen4 x4), with capacity between 250 GB and 8 TB, recommends a read speed of at least 5,500 MB/s and asks for a heatsink that fits the compartment. SATA SSDs and third-generation NVMe drives won't do.

An external USB drive also works, with a limit: it can store PS5 games and run PS4 games, while PS5 games have to be copied back to the SSD to play. It is a good solution for anyone who wants to archive their library without downloading everything again.

Xbox Series X and S: expansion card or USB

Xbox uses its own solution. The Storage Expansion Card, made by Seagate and Western Digital, plugs into a port on the back of the console and works like the internal SSD. An external USB 3.0 HDD or SSD of at least 128 GB runs Xbox One, Xbox 360 and original Xbox games, but only stores games optimized for Series X and S, which must go back to internal storage or the card to run.

Switch 2: microSD Express only

Switch 2 has the strictest rule. The console only accepts microSD Express cards, a newer standard that uses the same PCIe and NVMe technology as SSDs and reaches speeds close to 900 MB/s, against about 100 MB/s for regular microSD cards. The cards you used in the first Switch can't save games on the new console. According to Nintendo, they can only be used to import screenshots and videos taken on the previous Switch. Switch 2 accepts microSD Express cards up to 2 TB.

And on PC?

On a computer, the most common advice is to split the jobs. An NVMe SSD for the system and the games you are playing, and a bigger HDD for photos, videos, backups and games that can wait. Before buying, check the motherboard manual for how many M.2 slots it has and which PCIe generation they are. A Gen4 SSD works in a Gen3 slot, but is limited to the lower speed.

Two tips apply to every platform. First, the advertised capacity is never what's left free, because the system takes up part of the space. Second, an SSD left unused for a long time isn't the best place for the only backup of something important. For long-term archiving, keep copies in more than one place.
