280 Ice Age engravings went unnoticed for decades in a tourist cave in Catalonia

Horses, aurochs and even a bear were mixed in with names and dates scratched by visitors in the Cova de la Font Major. An archaeologist spotted them with a headlamp.

By João GabrielNovember 21, 2026, 14:16

Gravats a la Cova de la Font Major: un santuari paleolític! (IPHES-CERCA channel, in Catalan)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

The Cova de la Font Major, in Catalonia, in northeastern Spain, has had visitors for centuries. Even so, thousands of people walked past 280 engravings at least 19,000 years old without noticing. An archaeologist angled his headlamp sideways at the wall, saw what was there, and in 2026 the discovery was published in the journal Antiquity.

What was found

Researchers Josep Maria Vergès, of the IPHES-CERCA institute, and Míriam Salas-Altès catalogued 280 motifs engraved in the rock. Of these, 55 are animal figures: horses, goats, deer, cattle and a bear. The rest are abstract shapes and signs. According to IFLScience, it is the third largest set of Palaeolithic engravings in Mediterranean Iberia.

In one of the study's images, the conglomerate wall serves as the ground line for a horse, as if the artist had used the rock's own edge. In another, the front half of an aurochs, the extinct wild ox, appears with its horns and ears drawn from the front. There is also a panel marked by hundreds of loop-shaped engravings.

The images in the scientific paper come in pairs: the photograph and the tracing, a drawing that retraces only the engraved lines. The tracing helps because, in photos of engravings, the eye gets lost in the natural relief of the stone. With it, the outline of the animal stands out without noise, and other researchers can check what was interpreted as a figure.

Why nobody saw them before

The answer lies in the visiting itself. The cave has been frequented since the 12th century, according to Archaeology Magazine, and after 1957 it received tourists, explorers and treasure hunters. In 1993 it became an official tourist attraction. Over time, many visitors carved names, dates and messages on the same walls, and the ancient figures ended up mixed with these recent inscriptions. The engravings were only identified in 2019, according to Archaeology Magazine.

Vergès noticed them while looking at the graffiti with a headlamp at a sideways angle, IFLScience reports. The trick makes sense: raking light makes every shallow groove cast a tiny shadow, so the engraving shows up where, under head-on light, the wall looks smooth.

How old are they, really?

This is where it's worth separating what is confirmed from what is an estimate. Neither of the two reports mentions laboratory dating, such as radiocarbon. IFLScience says there are signs of human presence in the cave 40,000 and 26,000 years ago, and that the style of the engravings points to at least 19,000 years. Archaeology Magazine adds that many figures predate the Magdalenian period, the final phase of Ice Age art in Europe, but gives no dates in years. In other words: "19,000 years" is a floor estimated from style, not a measurement.

What this changes

The title of the study already points to the gap it wants to fill: "Filling gaps" in the Palaeolithic art of the northeast Iberian Peninsula. A place that thousands of people saw without seeing suggests that the map of this art still has blank areas, even in well-known sites with guided tours.

There is one detail that stings: the names scratched on the same walls, next to the ancient figures. For the public, the lesson is practical: a cave wall is not a notebook.

I couldn't open the full text of the study; the figures come from two reports that cite it, and both agree on the points above.
