Does the Switch 2 use two kinds of DLSS? What Digital Foundry found and what Nintendo hasn't confirmed

An October 2025 analysis points to a full model and a lighter one running on the console. Here is what that changes on screen and what is still guesswork.

By Aloha TeamNovember 11, 2026, 19:06

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

The Switch 2 is a handheld that also plays in 4K on the TV, and a good part of that trick depends on one acronym: DLSS. In October 2025, Digital Foundry showed evidence that the console uses two different models of the technology. To write this piece I read Nvidia's page, a Brazilian blog's analysis, Project N's report on Fallout 4 and three sites that described the Digital Foundry video (Nintendo Life, Tom's Hardware and Club386). I could not open the video itself, so everything I attribute to it comes from those texts.

What DLSS is, in plain English

The console draws the game at a lower resolution and a neural network, trained by Nvidia, rebuilds the image at a higher one. That takes less from the graphics chip than drawing everything in high resolution. Nvidia says, in an April 2025 text, that the Switch 2 processor is custom-made by it and has Tensor Cores, the units dedicated to this kind of artificial-intelligence calculation. The company promises games in up to 4K on the TV and up to 120 frames per second at 1080p in handheld mode.

What Digital Foundry found

According to the three sites, the analysis points to two models. The first resembles the CNN (convolutional neural network) model used on PCs before DLSS 4, delivers a steadier image and is limited to 1080p output. Nintendo Life cites Cyberpunk 2077: Ultimate Edition and Street Fighter 6 as examples. The second is a lighter version, which costs about half the processing time per frame and allows resolutions above 1080p. According to Nintendo Life, it shows up in Star Wars Outlaws, Hogwarts Legacy, The Touryst and Fast Fusion.

The price of the light version is sharpness in motion. Tom's Hardware and Club386 say it loses definition and can show unstable edges when the camera or objects move, and Club386 describes a clean image, close to 1440p or 4K, “only in the static parts”. Nintendo Life adds that presenter Alex Battaglia struggles to see a use for that model in TV mode.

And did Nintendo confirm it?

No. Nintendo Life writes that there has been no official word from Nintendo or Nvidia on the matter. The only backing beyond the analysis itself, according to the site, is an anonymous developer who confirmed to Digital Foundry that the theory is correct. The nicknames “DLSS Light”, “Fat DLSS” and “Tiny DLSS” are labels from the sites, not Nvidia names. The three texts also don't state the internal resolution at which the games are drawn before reconstruction.

What has already changed in practice

The most recent example I found is Fallout 4: Anniversary Edition. According to Project N, Bethesda released an update on March 26, 2026 that adds DLSS in the 40 and 60 frames-per-second modes. The feature turns itself off on screens full of interface, such as the Pip-Boy. Alexandre Porfírio's blog, from October 2026, also cites a February Digital Foundry analysis that pointed to DLSS in Resident Evil Requiem on the Switch 2, with limits in frame-rate stability. I only know that second case from that citation, without having read the original analysis.

DLSS is not the AI you might be thinking of

Porfírio's text makes a distinction worth repeating. DLSS is an image-reconstruction technique. It is not generative artificial intelligence, the kind that chats or creates content. He recalls that Nintendo president Shuntaro Furukawa spoke about AI at the June 2026 shareholders' meeting, acknowledging the potential of generative AI and concerns about intellectual property and energy consumption. According to the author, there is no confirmation of broad use of generative AI in the games.

What it means for players

In practice, the Switch 2's image quality will depend on each studio: which model to use, at what resolution and with what performance target. That explains why two games on the same console can look so different. When a comparison of a game you want comes out, it's worth finding out which of the two approaches it uses.

Sources: NVIDIA Brasil (in Portuguese), Nintendo Life, Tom's Hardware, Club386, Project N (in Portuguese) and Alexandre Porfírio (in Portuguese).
