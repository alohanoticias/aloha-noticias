Voyager 1 reaches one light-day from Earth on November 18: what it really means

When the probe crosses the mark, a radio signal will take 24 hours to reach it. Here is the math, the disagreement over dates and what still works on board.

By João GabrielNovember 16, 2026, 13:57

NASA's Voyager 1 is in Interstellar Space (NASA channel)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

On the morning of November 18, around 7 a.m. in Brasília, Voyager 1 is set to complete a measurement that sounds simple and isn't: sitting one light-day from Earth. A radio signal, traveling at the speed of light, will need 24 hours to get from here to the most distant probe humanity has ever put in space.

What a light-day is, in practice

It is the distance light covers in 24 hours: about 25.9 billion kilometers, or 173 times the distance between Earth and the Sun. A simple calculation checks out: 299,792 km per second times the 86,400 seconds in a day gives those 25.9 billion.

For scale, a light-year, the yardstick of the stars, is 365 light-days. The Sun's nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is about 4.2 light-years away, which is more than 1,500 light-days. Voyager 1, launched on September 5, 1977, took more than 49 years to cover the first one. That is less than a tenth of one percent of the way to that star, even though the probe isn't heading there: it is moving toward the constellation Ophiuchus.

What day, what time?

This is where the sources disagree, and it's worth saying why. IFLScience, which spoke with the mission team, gives November 18 at 10:16 UTC (7:16 a.m. in Brasília) and warns that the exact moment is only likely and may shift slightly because the probe and Earth are both moving. EarthSky also says November 18, but with a different time, 6:16 UTC. NASA's November sky page mentions the 17th. And Popular Science still carries November 15, in a projection that looks older than the rest (the text shows no publication date).

My reading: the crossing happens around the turn from the 17th to the 18th, and the exact hour isn't settled. If NASA publishes an official time, it outranks any of these.

A conversation that takes two days

The most concrete effect is the delay in conversation. Popular Science reports that each command already takes a little over 23 hours to travel from Earth to the probe, or back. If someone sent a "hi" and Voyager answered instantly, the reply would arrive only after about 48 hours. That is how the team works: send the command, then wait two days to find out what happened.

What still works on board

Not much. According to IFLScience, most instruments have been switched off to save power, cameras included, and the team is planning an intervention to extend the life of both Voyagers. EarthSky notes that NASA turned off the LECP instrument in April 2026 for the same reason. Power comes from three generators that use the heat of radioactive material and are expected to run out sometime in the 2030s, according to Popular Science. Last year, technical problems took weeks to fix.

Can you see Voyager?

No. It is tiny and far too distant for any backyard telescope. What NASA published is a chart showing the direction it lies in, in Ophiuchus, for anyone who wants to know where to point. That's all: the spot in the sky marks the direction, not the probe.

And Voyager 2?

It is about 3.2 billion kilometers closer to Earth and should reach one light-day in November 2035, according to EarthSky. Both carry a golden disc with sounds and images from Earth. Linda Spilker, a scientist on the Voyager project, summed up their fate to IFLScience: when the probes can no longer talk to us, those discs will be "silent ambassadors".
