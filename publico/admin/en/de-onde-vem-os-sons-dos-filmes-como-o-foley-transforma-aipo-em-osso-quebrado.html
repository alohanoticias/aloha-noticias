Where movie sounds come from: how Foley turns celery into breaking bones

Footsteps, clothing, punches and doors are rarely recorded during filming. A Foley artist creates almost all of it, in a studio full of old shoes and vegetables.

By João GabrielNovember 25, 2026, 18:03

Movie magic: how Foley artists create the sounds behind your favourite films (Euronews Culture)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Pay attention to the next fight scene you watch. The punch landing on a face, the coat brushing the wall, the footsteps down the hallway: almost none of it was recorded on the day of filming. Those sounds were made later, in a studio, by someone watching the scene on a screen and reproducing every movement with everyday objects. It is the work of the Foley artist, one of the most hidden jobs in film.

A name that became a profession

The term comes from Jack Foley, who lived from 1891 to 1967 and began recreating sounds for the first talking pictures in the late 1920s. Much of what he did was re-record the actors' footsteps, synchronized with the picture. Foley received almost no credit in his lifetime, but his surname ended up naming the craft, and his basic method is still in use.

Why not record everything on set?

Because a set is built to capture dialogue. The microphone follows the voices, and everything else comes out faint, muffled or mixed with noises that shouldn't be there, such as generators, traffic and crew. Recreating sounds in a studio gives control: every footstep becomes a separate file, with the right volume and tone for the scene.

There is also a reason that matters directly to audiences in countries like Brazil. For a film to be dubbed, the producer delivers a track with only music and effects, without the original lines. If a sound had been recorded together with the dialogue, it would vanish from the dubbed version. That is why many productions today Foley practically everything, precisely so that this international track is complete.

The trick is in the wrong object

The fun of Foley is that the right sound rarely comes from the object on screen. Some classic examples of the trade:

For breaking bones, artists twist and snap celery stalks or frozen lettuce. A punch to the face often comes from a cabbage struck hard. Footsteps in snow can be cornstarch squeezed inside a small leather pouch. Rain is rice falling on a metal sheet, and the crackle of fire comes from cellophane crumpled near the microphone.

Footsteps are taken seriously: the artist wears a shoe similar to the character's and walks on the right surface, whether wood, gravel or asphalt. In the United States, people who specialize in this are called "Foley walkers".

The three kinds of sound

The work is usually split into three families. Footsteps, which follow every character on screen. Movement, such as clothing, leather and the rustle of a coat. And props, which cover everything that is touched, opened, broken or thrown.

In animation the challenge is even bigger: since there is no production sound at all, every step and every brush of fabric has to be created from scratch.

And in Brazil?

Foley has a history in Brazil too. A University of São Paulo dissertation devoted to the subject follows the activity in the country since the 1950s, along with the main studios and artists working today. Among the best-known names in Brazilian sound post-production is Alessandro Laroca, who worked on films such as City of God and Elite Squad.

A test for your next screening

Pick a calm scene with no music and listen only to the details: the cup touching the saucer, the creaking chair, the hand running through hair. In almost every case, someone made that sound with their own hands while watching a screen.
