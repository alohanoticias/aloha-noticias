Manga, light novel, web novel and anime: where the story you're watching comes from

Almost every anime of the season adapts something published before. Understanding the difference between formats helps you know where to continue the story when the season ends.

By João GabrielNovember 26, 2026, 09:58

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

When an anime season ends, the most common question in the comments is always the same: where can I continue the story? The answer depends on where it came from. The vast majority of anime adapt a work published earlier, and that work can be a manga, a light novel or even a text posted for free online. Each format has its own pace, audience and path to becoming animation.

Manga: the magazine comes before the book

Manga is Japanese comics, read from right to left. In Japan, most series are born in weekly or monthly magazines, which publish one chapter per issue of several titles at once. The most famous is Shueisha's Weekly Shonen Jump, which published series such as One Piece, Naruto and Jujutsu Kaisen.

When there are enough chapters, they are collected into bound volumes, the tankobon, which are the small books sold in stores and that reach Brazil through publishers such as Panini and JBC. That's why the release pace in Japan is always ahead of Brazil's, and the anime usually follows that chapter order closely.

Light novel: the illustrated novel

A light novel is a prose novel written for a young audience, with an anime-style cover and a few illustrations inside. The most common format is bunko, a small paperback, published by specialized imprints of the big Japanese publishers. It reads quickly, with lots of dialogue and short chapters.

Many fantasy and science fiction anime come from there. An important difference for anyone who wants to continue the story: since each volume has a lot of text, a season usually covers only part of the series, and many end with most of the books still unadapted. That's why anyone who finishes the anime usually has plenty of material ahead.

Web novel: it all starts for free online

Over the past 15 years, a huge share of successful light novels started in an even simpler way: as text posted for free on a website. The main one is Shōsetsuka ni Narō, whose name means "let's become novelists", online since 2004. There, anyone can post chapters, and the most-read stories catch publishers' attention.

Re:Zero is the classic example. Tappei Nagatsuki began posting the story on the site in April 2012, and the print version came out in 2014 under the MF Bunko J imprint, with revised text and illustrations. Only then came the anime. That Time I Got Reincarnated as a Slime, Mushoku Tensei and Overlord followed the same path. The site has already passed the mark of 200 works adapted into anime, and much of the wave of isekai stories, in which the protagonist ends up in another world, was born there.

Why does so much anime come from another work?

Making anime is expensive and risky. Most series are financed by a production committee, a group of companies that splits costs and profits: the publisher of the original work, the studio, TV channels, streaming platforms, record labels and toy makers. For the publisher, the anime works as a big advertisement, because book sales usually rise when the series airs. Adapting a story that already has an audience lowers the risk for everyone.

Where to continue after the season

The first step is to find out the series' source, information that appears in the opening credits or on the official page. If it's manga, check whether there's a local edition and at which volume the anime stopped. If it's a light novel, the offer in Portuguese is smaller, and the alternative is usually the official English edition. Keep in mind that when an anime catches up with the source material, a continuation can take years, because the studio has to wait for the story to advance on paper.
