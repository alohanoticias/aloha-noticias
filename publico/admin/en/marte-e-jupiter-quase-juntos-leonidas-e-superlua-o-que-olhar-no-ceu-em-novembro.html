Mars and Jupiter almost together, the Leonids and a supermoon: what to look for in the November sky

Dates from NASA and Star Walk, what changes for viewers in Brazil, and what to expect from each event without setting the bar too high.

By João GabrielNovember 13, 2026, 14:04

What's Up: November 2026 Skywatching Tips from NASA (NASA JPL channel)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

November has three events that are easy to watch from your doorstep and one that takes patience. Mars and Jupiter come close to each other on the mornings of the 15th and 16th, the Leonids peak on the 17th and 18th, and the supermoon arrives on the 24th. I put together the dates from NASA and Star Walk and checked what each one lets you see from Brazil.

One caveat first: NASA's guide is written for the Northern Hemisphere. When it tells you to look southeast, for instance, the position in Brazil shifts a little. A sky-map app sorts that out in two minutes.

Mars and Jupiter, 1.2 degrees apart

On the mornings of November 15 and 16, before sunrise, the two planets sit about 1.2 degrees apart. Jupiter is the brightest point; Mars, reddish, is next to it. Both are visible to the naked eye. A simple calculation helps picture it: the full Moon spans about half a degree of sky, so the gap between the two planets is a little over two Moons side by side. NASA's chart also shows Venus low on the horizon, in the same stretch of sky.

Leonids: the event that depends most on luck

The meteor shower is active from November 6 to 30 and comes from the debris of comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle. NASA and Star Walk agree on the main points: a peak on the nights of the 17th and 18th, with at most 15 meteors per hour under ideal conditions. Most people will see fewer than that.

For viewers in Brazil there are two complications. The first is the time: the International Meteor Organization's forecast for the maximum is 8:45 p.m. Brasília time on the 17th, and another forecast cited by Star Walk says 1 p.m. the same day, which means daylight. The second is that, from here, the point the meteors seem to radiate from, in the constellation Leo, sits lower, especially in the south. Both sources recommend watching after the Moon sets and before dawn, in the early hours of the 18th, with your eyes adapted to the dark for about 30 minutes. Check when the Moon sets in your city, because neither source gives that number.

Supermoon on November 24

The full Moon on the 24th is the first of three consecutive supermoons, followed by December and January, according to NASA. Near the horizon it tends to look bigger, but that is an illusion that applies to any full Moon, NASA says.

The Moon calendar, day by day

From NASA's table: new Moon on November 8, first quarter on the 17th, full Moon on the 24th and last quarter on the 30th. The times are in Pacific time, so near the turn of the day the date can differ by one day in Brazil. In practice, on the night of the Leonids the Moon will be at first quarter and only clears the sky once it sets.

What is still uncertain

The forecast time for the Leonids changes from source to source, and clouds decide the rest. For the planets the window is short: NASA mentions only the mornings of the 15th and 16th. For the meteors, it is worth trying more than one night around the 17th and 18th, since the shower is active from November 6 to 30.
