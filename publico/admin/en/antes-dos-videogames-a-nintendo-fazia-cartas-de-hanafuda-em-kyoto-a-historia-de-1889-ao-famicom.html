Before video games, Nintendo made hanafuda cards in Kyoto: the story from 1889 to the Famicom

The company was born on 23 September 1889, making Japanese playing cards by hand. What is known, where the sources disagree, and what is legend.

By Aloha TeamNovember 6, 2026, 13:14

ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

On 23 September 1889, in Kyoto, Fusajiro Yamauchi opened a factory making handmade cards. The date appears in five of the sources I read, Wikipedia and MoneyWeek among them, and Nintendo itself confirms the year 1889 on the history page of its Japanese website. The product was hanafuda, and Nintendo grew out of it.

What hanafuda cards are

Hanafuda means “flower cards.” The deck has 48 cards split into 12 suits of four, one for each month of the year, with flowers, animals and landscapes in place of European numbers and suits. According to Wikipedia, Japan banned most gambling in 1882 but tolerated hanafuda, and Kyoto decks began supplying gambling houses. The sources differ on the details of the law: in another entry, Wikipedia speaks of rules being relaxed in 1885. Also according to Wikipedia, Yamauchi played hanafuda and watched the business grow, and that is what led him to open the factory.

The name nobody can explain

In Japanese, Nintendo is written 任天堂. A translation often repeated is “leave luck to heaven.” Wikipedia says this explanation has no historical backing and that not even Yamauchi's descendants know the original meaning; Illuminatti Games, in Portuguese, likewise treats it as traditional but unproven. MoneyWeek and Memória BIT don't even try to explain it. It remains a widely accepted legend.

1902 or 1907? The first Western card

The company's official page says that in 1902 it began making and selling the first Western-style cards in Japan. Wikipedia notes that this is Nintendo's version, that other documents point to 1907, and it also cites a 1974 company leaflet that gives 1911. This is no minor detail: Western decks became the firm's livelihood when a 1902 tax doubled the price of most hanafuda decks, according to Wikipedia. So we are left with a date the company states and other documents do not confirm.

Plastic and Disney

Hiroshi Yamauchi became president in 1950, according to Wikipedia, and in 1951 the company was renamed Nintendo Playing Card Co., Ltd. In 1953 it became the first in Japan to produce plastic cards on an industrial scale (Nintendo and Wikipedia agree), and in 1959 it struck a deal with Disney to put the characters on its decks. According to Wikipedia, by 1961 it had sold more than 1.5 million packs. The company became Nintendo Co., Ltd. in 1963.

When the deck was no longer enough

Wikipedia says that with the Disney decks Nintendo grew dependent on the children's market, and that in 1965 it hired Gunpei Yokoi to look after the card-assembly machines. In 1969 it created its research and development department. The Ultra Hand, from 1966, sold more than a million units, according to Wikipedia and DicasPC. In 1970 the Beam Gun, described by Wikipedia as Japan's first electronic toy, also passed that mark.

Taxis, a love hotel and instant rice also appear in this period in some texts. That shows up only in DicasPC; I found nothing of the sort on Nintendo's page, on Wikipedia or in the other texts. So I assert nothing.

From toys to the Famicom

In 1977 came the first home consoles, the TV Game 15 and the TV Game 6, according to Nintendo. In 1980, the Game & Watch, created by Yokoi using calculator technology, which Wikipedia says sold 43.4 million units. In July 1983 the Famicom arrived in Japan, a date given by Wikipedia and DicasPC.

Does Nintendo still make hanafuda? MoneyWeek, in a text updated in 2020, says it does, alongside other traditional card decks and board games for the Japanese market. Illuminatti Games and DicasPC point the same way, and the latter mentions editions with Mario and Zelda themes in the official store in Japan. None of this could be checked against the company's official page.

Sources: Nintendo (history), Wikipedia: Nintendo, Wikipedia: Fusajiro Yamauchi, MoneyWeek, Memória BIT (in Portuguese), Illuminatti Games (in Portuguese) and DicasPC (in Portuguese).
