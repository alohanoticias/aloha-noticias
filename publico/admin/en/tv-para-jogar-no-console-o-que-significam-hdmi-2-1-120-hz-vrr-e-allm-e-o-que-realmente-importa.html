A TV for console gaming: what HDMI 2.1, 120 Hz, VRR and ALLM mean, and what really matters

The "HDMI 2.1" label on the box guarantees nothing by itself. Here's what each acronym does, what PS5, Xbox Series and Switch 2 actually use, and what to check before buying.

By Aloha TeamNovember 17, 2026, 14:37

HDMI 2.1 explained (Mundo Conectado, in Portuguese)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Anyone shopping for a new TV to play games on finds an alphabet soup in the spec sheet: HDMI 2.1, 120 Hz, VRR, ALLM, eARC. Salespeople treat it all as if it were the same thing, and it isn't. Some of these features make a visible difference with a PS5 or an Xbox Series X. Others are irrelevant for the console you own. And the most famous label of all, HDMI 2.1, can be on the box of a TV that offers none of the features that made it famous.

Why isn't "HDMI 2.1" on the box enough?

Since late 2021, the body that licenses the standard, HDMI Licensing Administrator, stopped certifying products as HDMI 2.0. Under the current rule, the 2.0 features became a subset of 2.1, and all the new 2.1 features are optional. In practice, a TV can be sold as "HDMI 2.1" even without delivering 120 Hz at 4K or variable refresh rate. The body itself advises manufacturers to state which features each port supports.

The takeaway for buyers is easy to apply: ignore the version number and look for the features by name in the spec sheet. Another detail that catches many people: on several TVs, only one or two of the HDMI ports have the full feature set. The console needs to go into exactly those.

120 Hz: more frames per second

Refresh rate is how many times per second the screen draws a new image. Most TVs run at 60 Hz. A 120 Hz panel can show up to 120 frames per second, which makes motion smoother and cuts the delay between pressing a button and seeing the response. PS5 and Xbox Series X have games with 120-frame modes, and Switch 2 offers 120 frames on the TV when resolution is set to 1080p or 1440p.

Watch out for marketing: terms like "Motion Rate 120" or "effective 120 Hz" usually signal image processing on top of a 60 Hz panel. What matters is the panel's native rate, which shows up in the full specifications or in independent reviews.

VRR: the TV follows the game's rhythm

VRR, short for variable refresh rate, makes the TV adjust its frequency to the pace at which the console delivers frames. When a game swings between 45 and 60 frames, for example, the image doesn't "tear" and stutters become much less noticeable. PS5 gained the feature in an April 2022 update and requires a TV with VRR over HDMI 2.1. Xbox Series X and S have supported it since launch.

Switch 2 is the most recent case. At launch, Nintendo limited VRR to the handheld's screen. System update 23.0.0, from September 2026, brought the feature to TV mode, but only in games that developers enable, and both the console and the dock need to be updated.

ALLM and game mode: less lag

TVs process the image to make it look nicer in movies, and that processing creates lag. Game mode turns most of it off. ALLM, short for auto low latency mode, makes the TV switch to that mode by itself when it detects a console. If yours lacks ALLM, just turn on game mode manually in the picture settings. It is the setting with the biggest impact and the most forgotten one.

What to check before buying

Build the list around the console you use. For PS5 or Xbox Series X, prioritize a native 120 Hz panel, VRR and at least one port with 4K at 120 Hz. For Xbox Series S, Switch 2 or PS4, 120 Hz at 4K matters less, and the essential thing is a good low-latency game mode. The cables that come with PS5 and Xbox Series X are already Ultra High Speed. If you need another, look for that certification label on the package. And if you'll connect a soundbar, check that the eARC port isn't the same one you set aside for the console. Anyone building a gaming setup from scratch can also check what Valve has confirmed about the Steam Machine.
