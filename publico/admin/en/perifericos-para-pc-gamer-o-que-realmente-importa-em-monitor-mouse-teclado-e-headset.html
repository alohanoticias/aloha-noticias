Gaming PC peripherals: what really matters in a monitor, mouse, keyboard and headset

Sky-high DPI, RGB lights and 8,000 Hz fill the boxes, but little of it changes the game. Here's what to prioritize in each piece and in what order to spend.

By Aloha TeamNovember 26, 2026, 18:43

How to choose your next gaming mouse (Canaltech, in Portuguese)
ShareWhatsAppXFacebookTelegram

Building a gaming PC usually takes all the attention toward the graphics card and processor, and the peripherals are left for last, with whatever money is left. It's a common mistake, because they are what you touch and look at the whole time. The other mistake is the opposite: paying a lot for numbers that look impressive on the box and make little difference in practice. This guide separates one from the other, piece by piece, starting where the investment pays off most.

Monitor: the piece that changes the experience the most

If the budget is tight, the monitor is usually the best place to put the money. A 144 Hz or faster model shows twice as many frames as a regular 60 Hz monitor, and the difference shows in any action game, not just competitive ones. That only makes sense if the PC can deliver that many frames, so it's worth looking at both together.

Resolution should match screen size. At 24 inches, 1080p still works well. At 27 inches, 1440p makes the image sharper without demanding as much from the graphics card as 4K. Support for FreeSync or G-Sync, the variable refresh rate technologies for monitors, helps avoid tearing when performance fluctuates.

Mouse: shape and sensor before DPI

DPI measures how many points the sensor records per inch of movement. Numbers like 26,000 or 30,000 are marketing: most players use between 400 and 1,600 DPI and adjust sensitivity in the game. What matters is the quality of the sensor, which has to keep up with fast movements without skipping, and any gaming mouse from a well-known brand already delivers that.

The deciding factor is shape. People who rest their whole hand on the mouse prefer larger, taller models. Those who play with only their fingertips tend to like small, light mice. Ideally, hold it before buying. As for polling rate, 1,000 Hz means the mouse reports its position to the PC every millisecond, and that is already enough for almost everyone. Wireless mice with their own 2.4 GHz receiver have latency comparable to a cable, unlike regular Bluetooth.

Keyboard: the switch defines the feel

In mechanical keyboards, each key has its own switch, and it defines how the key responds. Linear models, usually red, go down smoothly with no resistance halfway. Tactile ones, usually brown, have a small bump that signals when the key has registered. Clicky ones, usually blue, add an audible click to that bump, great for typing and terrible for anyone sharing a room or on voice calls.

In recent years, keyboards with magnetic switches have gained ground, letting you choose at what point in the press the key registers. They are prized in competitive shooters, but aren't needed to start. For most people, a regular mechanical keyboard or even a good membrane keyboard does the job. Compact layouts, without the number pad, free up desk space for the mouse.

Headset: the microphone matters most

In online games, hearing where footsteps come from helps, and any decent stereo headphone already delivers that. The virtual surround sound many headsets advertise depends a lot on the game and personal taste. In practice, the difference others notice most is the microphone: a headset with poor pickup bothers the whole team. An alternative many people adopt is using good regular headphones with a separate microphone.

In what order to spend

For anyone building everything from scratch, a sensible order is monitor first, then mouse, keyboard and finally headset. A large, good-quality mousepad is the cheapest purchase on the list and often improves aim more than an expensive mouse. And RGB lights, however pretty, don't improve performance at all.
